What separates a good prompt from a bad one

Most people type a wish and get a guess back. "Write a YouTube script" is a wish. The model has no idea who is watching, how long the video should be, or what the channel sounds like, so it produces the most average version of a script it can find. That average output is why so much AI writing reads the same.

A prompt that works carries five things: a role, a task, the audience, the constraints, and the output shape. Miss any one and the model fills the gap with a default you did not choose.

  • Role — who the model should be while answering. "You are a scriptwriter for a Hindi finance channel" is different from "You are a scriptwriter."
  • Task — the single thing you want produced. One task per prompt. If you ask for a script and a thumbnail idea and a title, you usually get three shallow answers.
  • Audience — who reads or watches it. Age, city, what they already know, what they are worried about.
  • Constraints — length, tone, language, what to avoid. "No jargon, under 140 words, Hinglish, do not promise returns" is a constraint list that changes the output completely.
  • Output shape — the exact structure you want back. "Give it as a table with three columns" or "give me five options, each under 60 characters."

The same request, written properly

Here is the weak version:

Write a YouTube script about saving money.

Here is the same request with all five parts:

You are a scriptwriter for a personal-finance channel aimed at salaried Indians aged 25-35.

Write a 4-minute YouTube script on why a ₹5,000 monthly SIP beats a one-time ₹1 lakh investment.

Rules:
- Hinglish, conversational, no jargon
- Open with a hook under 15 seconds that names a real worry
- Use one concrete rupee example, not abstract percentages
- Do not promise returns or give investment advice
- End with one clear action

Format: hook, then three sections with a one-line heading each, then the action.

The second version tells the model what to be, what to write, who reads it, what to avoid and how to lay it out. The difference in output is not small — it is the difference between something you can post and something you have to rewrite from scratch.

Prompts for AI images work differently

Text models can work from a description. Image models need a picture in words, and they care about things a writer would never mention: the lens, the light, the framing, the background.

A usable image prompt names the subject, then the setting, then the light, then the style, then the technical detail. "A chai stall" is not a prompt. "A small roadside chai stall at dawn, steel kettle steaming, one customer on a stool, warm low side-light, 50mm lens, shallow depth of field, film grain" is a prompt — every clause removes a choice the model would otherwise make for you.

Two habits help. Put the things you care about most at the front, because earlier words carry more weight. And when a result is nearly right, change one clause at a time instead of rewriting the whole prompt, so you learn which words actually did the work.

Four mistakes that waste your free generations

  • Asking for everything at once. One prompt, one output. If you need a title, a script and a caption, that is three prompts, and each one will be better than a combined request.
  • Never saying what to avoid. Models default to filler — "in today's fast-paced world", "game-changer", "unlock your potential". Naming those as banned removes them.
  • Accepting the first answer. Ask for five options and pick one. The first output is the most predictable one the model can produce.
  • Not saving what worked. When a prompt produces something you actually used, keep it. A personal library of ten good prompts is worth more than a thousand generic ones.
